The Exposures, Impacts, and Strategies Inventory for Extreme Heat is an online tool that guides California Tribes through a structured extreme heat vulnerability assessment. It is a collaborative pilot led by the Pala Band of Mission Indians, Prosper Sustainably and the Public Health Alliance of Southern California, funded through the California Energy Commission’s Tribal Research Grant Program. The work took the EISI framework — an established method for assessing climate-related risk, developed by the Tribal Climate Health Project — and rebuilt it as an interactive, guided application on top of the California Healthy Places Index: Extreme Heat Edition map platform. It was developed against a standing cohort of Tribal advisors who reviewed each stage.

The problem the tool addresses is one of access rather than analysis. The projected heat and vulnerability data a Tribe needs already exists, but assembling it for a specific territory, interpreting it against local conditions and turning it into a defensible written assessment assumes staff time and technical expertise that a small, often understaffed Tribal department does not have spare. Advisors were consistent about where the value lands: the assessment is what a funder asks for, and having one in hand lets a Tribe speak the language grant reviewers expect and show that climate risk has been properly considered.

The tool runs as a guided inventory. A Tribe defines its area, reviews primary and secondary exposures, catalogues the community assets it wants to protect, ranks the impacts that follow, and arrives at a community profile and an exported report. At the exposure steps it presents the underlying climate data directly: annual days above ninety and a hundred degrees, extreme heat days above the historical baseline, heat health events, warm nights — each across a historical baseline, a current period and mid- and end-century projections, with a trend line. The Tribe reads that evidence and then sets the risk level itself, from none through to high, because the data describes the exposure while only the community can weigh what it means locally. Assets and impacts are ranked on the same scale, and a community profile assembles itself in a panel beside the form so the document can be seen taking shape rather than arriving as a surprise at the end.

The output is the point, and it is deliberately not a finished document. The tool generates an editable Word file, colour-coded so a Tribe can see at a glance what came from where: one colour for values drawn straight from the assessment, another for standing background text on extreme heat that is theirs to rewrite, another again for prompts suggesting where local data, traditional ecological knowledge and community engagement belong. It opens with an executive summary and a table of the high and medium risks identified, then works through findings — observations, threatened assets, and vulnerability across the health and social, natural and built environments — to recommendations. The risk table is expandable by design, because it can only assemble the impacts the data could reach on its own, and the report says so plainly and asks the Tribe to add what it knows.

That framing runs through the whole design. The tool is built to be completed with community input rather than instead of it, and ships alongside survey templates a Tribe can take into a workshop to establish which assets its own members most want protected. Where the underlying data has limits, the assessment names them: it is built on Healthy Places Index data with census tracts as the atomic unit, so a Tribe's territory is approximated from tract combinations and reservation boundaries do not always align cleanly; emergency room figures may not capture visits to Indian Health Service clinics, so the report directs Tribes to supplement from their own health facilities; and the data refreshes on a roughly five-year cycle rather than continuously. Being explicit about all of this is what makes the output usable as evidence rather than merely impressive.

The application is built on Next.js and Payload CMS with Vercel Postgres, using Mapbox GL for the mapping and Chakra UI for the interface, behind accounts with role-based permissions. Assessments are held as separate inventories, so work for different territories or planning cycles stays distinct — useful given that vulnerability assessments are themselves revisited on about a five-year rhythm, and a lighter update path is easier than a fresh overhaul. The pilot is scoped to extreme heat, with the framework designed to extend to further exposures — drought, wildfire and sea-level rise among them — without rebuilding the assessment flow.
